Chapter 1The Next Thing
 

The Presentation

Elias Vale gave the kind of presentation people remembered.

Not because he spoke loudly. He rarely did. They remembered him because he always found the thing everyone else had missed - the risk buried on page seventeen, the assumption no one had questioned.

The conference room was glass on two sides, city on both. Tuesday, 6:45 in the evening. The sky outside had gone the color of wet stone. Inside, the long table was clean, the water glasses were full, and on the screen behind him was a single slide with three lines on it.

He had removed forty-one slides that afternoon.

"What we lose," Elias said, "is not time. It's attention."

He clicked. The clicker hesitated - low battery - then worked. He filed it away without breaking rhythm. He always filed things away.

For twenty minutes he spoke without notes. He showed them the risk hidden inside the plan everyone liked. He showed them the cost of moving forward without asking the harder question. He showed them a simpler path. Nine months saved. Most of the budget protected. No drama, and no raised voice. He never needed one.

He held the room so completely he did not feel his own phone vibrate twice in his pocket. Whatever it was could wait. Everything could wait when there was a problem to solve.

When he finished, no one spoke for a moment.

Then the CEO stood up.

"This," she said, "is why we trust you, Elias. You see what others miss."

People applauded. Elias smiled - not proud, not humble, the smile of someone who knew he belonged. He felt the small plastic clicker grow light in his hand as the red low-battery light blinked, and he made a note in his mind: replace batteries before tomorrow.

Chapter 1

The Junior Analyst

A junior analyst waited while the room emptied. Young, eager, tie a little too tight, notebook full of notes he had copied word for word.

"Mr. Vale?" he said. "Sorry - I just wanted to say. I hope I have your career someday."

Elias stopped. Most senior people would have nodded and kept walking. He asked the boy's name.

"Milo."

"How long have you been with us, Milo?"

"Three months, sir."

Elias put a hand briefly on his shoulder. It was what a senior person was supposed to do, but he meant it.

"Work hard," he said, "and you will."

Milo grinned and walked away lighter, as if he had been given something to carry him for years. Elias watched him go and heard his own sentence follow him down the hall, and for half a second he did not know if he believed it.

Then he decided that he did. Of course he did. That was how it worked.

He kept walking.

Chapter 1

Drinks, Then the Violin

Afterward there were drinks.

"Come for one," said Priya from Legal. She had known him eight years, teased him for six of them. "You never come."

"I have the Mercer report," Elias said. "Tomorrow morning."

"You always have tomorrow morning." She smiled to soften it. "Do you ever turn off?"

Elias laughed. Everyone laughed. He liked them, and they knew it, and they kept inviting him. He lifted his bag a little, as proof, as apology, and left them to the elevator and the bar and the evening.

Outside the building, a man played violin near the subway entrance. He was good. He played something slow that belonged more to a concert hall than to wet concrete and commuters. People passed around him like water around a stone. Elias passed too. He was already answering an email with his thumb - Good point, let's tighten section two - and by the time he looked up, the music was behind him.

He had not heard a single note.

In the elevator up to the lobby, the air smelled, for half a second, of salt. Elias looked up from his phone. Nothing. He went back to the screen.

Chapter 1

The Apartment

His apartment was on the fourteenth floor. It was clean in the way hotel rooms are clean: perfect, ready for someone important.

He dropped his keys in the ceramic bowl by the door. He loosened his tie. He stood for a moment in the quiet.

Everything was where it belonged.

In the corner stood a guitar. He had bought it three years ago after watching a man play in a park on his lunch break. The man had looked free, and Elias had bought the guitar that same afternoon. He had told himself he would learn on weekends. The plastic wrap was still on the strings. Tonight there was a thin layer of dust on the case. He wiped it off with his thumb without thinking, the way you wipe a screen.

On the shelf were four travel books: Japan, Italy, Iceland, Greece. The spines were uncracked. He had marked pages with thin sticky notes - temples, coastlines, small restaurants - and he had never once written down the name of a hotel.

On the wall was a framed photograph. Five young people on a beach, sunburned and laughing, arms over shoulders. Elias in the middle, thinner, hair longer, holding a bottle like a trophy. One of the faces on the edge had gone soft in his memory. He could see the boy standing there, but he could not find his name anymore. Taped to the back of the frame, where no one could see it, was a note in faded ink:

Best summer of my life.

It was the summer before he had started his career.

He made coffee while answering two emails, and drank it answering a third, and set the empty cup down and did not remember tasting it.

He sat on the couch and opened his laptop. The cursor blinked. He closed his eyes for just a moment -

- and when he opened them, his thumb was already on the phone.

After this project, he thought, I will rest.

It was an old thought. He had thought it at twenty-two, when he wanted a good company. At twenty-seven, when he wanted to be senior. At thirty-two, when he wanted to be partner. Now he was thirty-four, and the thought had changed shape slightly:

Once I have financial freedom, I will live differently.

There was always another project.

Chapter 1

The Call

At 11:38 p.m. the phone rang.

Unknown number.

Elias glanced at his calendar for tomorrow before he answered, already calculating what would have to move.

"Mr. Vale?" a voice said. "This is Daniel Hart, attorney for Hart and Lane. I'm calling about your uncle. Thomas Vale."

Elias sat up straighter. He had not heard the name spoken aloud in years.

"He passed away last week," the lawyer said. "I'm sorry."

Elias waited for the grief. What came first was stranger: his uncle's hands on an old radio, patient, turning a dial no one else was listening to.

"He left you his property in Bellweather. The lighthouse."

Bellweather. Salt air and gull cries, twenty-two years away.

"I see," Elias said. "What are the conditions?"

"Condition, singular. You must restore it before sale. He was specific about that, and he was unusually specific, for him. It's a condition of transfer - the title doesn't come across until the executor has something in front of him that says the work's been done. I can send you the wording. It's not very interesting."

"He left a letter for you as well. It'll arrive by post, but I can tell you now - the place needs work. It's been years."

"Why would he leave it to me?" Elias said, before he could stop himself.

There was a pause.

"He said you would know what to do with something useless."

Elias did not know what to say to that.

After the call he stood at the window. The city below was still lit, still moving. Somewhere north, on a coast he had not visited since he was twelve, stood a lighthouse that had been dark for years.

When Elias was twelve, his parents had taken him to Bellweather once. He remembered climbing cold stone steps. At the top, his uncle had stood quietly, looking out at the water.

"Do you hear it?" he had asked.

Elias had listened. Wind. Waves. Gulls. Nothing else.

"Hear what?"

His uncle had smiled.

"Exactly."

At the time Elias had thought the man was strange.

Chapter 1

The Letter

The letter arrived Friday. Plain envelope. His name in uneven handwriting.

Inside, a single page.

Elias, I know you will try to solve this quickly. You have always been good at solving things. But not everything valuable is a problem. Come to Bellweather. Stay long enough to notice something. Thomas

Elias read it twice. He waited for the wise part. It did not come.

He set the letter on the table and opened his laptop to return to normal. Three unread emails.

The third had the subject line: Senior Partner Track - Recommendation.

He stared at it. It was the thing he had worked toward for years. His name, put forward. The next version of his life, ready to begin.

He looked at the letter. He looked at the email.

He did neither.

He went to bed. He set his alarm for 6:30, as always. Then he looked at it - Saturday, no meetings, no obligations - and did something he had not done in years.

He removed the alarm.

He lay in the dark of his perfect apartment. The guitar stood silent in the corner. The travel books sat unopened on the shelf. Milo's voice came back to him: I hope I have your career someday. The CEO's voice: You see what others miss. The violin he had walked past without hearing.

Anna on a train three years ago, saying you don't have to decide tonight, and him saying he would decide tonight, and meaning it.

For the first time in years, Elias had nothing to accomplish tomorrow.

The thought should have felt like freedom.

Instead, it felt like standing at the edge of an unfamiliar ocean.

He reached over in the dark and put his hand on the guitar case. He did not open it. He just held it, the way you hold a key to a room you are not yet ready to enter.

In the morning, without knowing why, he booked a one-way ticket to Bellweather.

Chapter 2North
 

The Leaving

"A week," Elias said. "Ten days max."

He had said it four times before nine in the morning, and each time it sounded slightly less true. It was Monday. The ticket north was for tomorrow. Nobody had asked him to explain, which made it worse - he kept offering the duration like a receipt, unprompted, to anyone who paused near his desk.

Milo found him by the elevators, breathless, as if he had rehearsed this. The notebook was under his arm - the same one, Elias noticed, full of notes copied word for word. Three months in and the boy still wrote down everything, the way Elias once had.

"Mr. Vale - is it true? About the partner track?"

Elias felt the question land somewhere low. The boy's eyes were bright with the particular hunger of someone who still believed the ladder led somewhere.

"Still in discussion," Elias said. "These things take time."

It was technically accurate. He had not withdrawn. He had simply stopped opening the email, the way you stop opening a door you intend to walk through later. Milo nodded as if he had been handed wisdom.

Priya caught his sleeve on the way out. She studied him the way lawyers study witnesses - fondly, and without missing anything.

"Family matter?" she said.

"Something like that."

She nodded, accepting the evasion the way old friends do. Then: "Bring me back a rock or something. Proof you looked down."

He laughed. "A rock."

"A rock," she confirmed, and let go of his sleeve.

On his laptop, the promotion thread sat at three unread. He closed the lid without opening them. The elevator doors closed on the office - the glass, the city, the long table where he had once explained attention to forty people - and for the first time in twelve years, Elias Vale left work in the middle of a project with nothing finished.

Chapter 2

The Packing

Elias stood in his apartment that evening with an open bag and found he had no practice at this kind of packing. Every trip he had ever taken had a client, an agenda, a return date circled in red. This bag had none of those. He packed the way he did everything: efficiently, and slightly too much.

The guitar stayed. He did not touch it. Three nights ago his hand had rested on its case in the dark; tonight he walked past it the way you pass a person you owe an apology - quickly, with your eyes elsewhere.

The photograph stayed. He did not turn the frame over. The note on the back could wait. Everything in this apartment was good at waiting; it had learned from him.

Thomas's letter went into the inside pocket of his jacket. He folded it and did not open it again. He checked that it was there twice - pat, pat - the way other men check for wallets. It would travel against his chest all the way north. He had already memorized the seven lines. Memorizing, he told himself, was the same as understanding.

At the bottom of his work bag, under the laptop, his fingers found the dead clicker. The red battery light was dark now. It was entirely useless - a small plastic husk that had once commanded rooms. He almost threw it away. Instead he slipped it into his coat pocket, for fidget. His hands had never learned to be empty.

The bag closed. The apartment locked behind him. Four travel books sat unopened on the shelf. Five days ago that had seemed normal. Tonight, for half a second, it did not.

Then he decided that it did, and went down to the street.

Chapter 2

The Train

The train left the city at 7:40 in the morning, and by 7:52 Elias had checked his phone eleven times. There was email, of course - there is always email - but the signal thinned as the buildings fell away, and by the first frozen field it was gone entirely. He checked again anyway. Then again. The gesture had detached from purpose years ago; his thumb moved the way a heart beats, without asking permission.

Across the aisle, an older woman unpacked groceries with the ceremony of someone setting a table: bread, cheese, an apple polished on her sleeve. Two seats down, a man slept with his mouth open, unbothered by witnesses. Outside, the frozen fields gave way to low woods, and the woods to hills. Nobody on this train was going anywhere important at speed. She caught him watching and smiled without surprise.

"Going home?" she said.

Elias heard himself answer before he had chosen one. "Something like that."

She nodded and looked out her own window, and asked nothing further. The conversation was over. It had lasted eleven seconds. He could not have said why it stayed with him past the next town: going home, going home, something like that.

He took the dead clicker from his coat pocket and turned it over in his hands. Click - nothing. Click - nothing. The button still traveled its small distance and returned, faithful and pointless. He clicked it through two stations. Somewhere past the river, with the hills beginning, he set it down on the fold-down tray to look out the window - and never picked it up again.

The hills were ordinary. That was the strange part. No grandeur, no revelation: low green rises, sheep like punctuation marks, a farmhouse with smoke going straight up in the windless air. He looked at them for a long time. Nobody needed him to have an opinion about the hills. The thought arrived quietly and stayed: nothing here requires solving.

It should have felt like wasted time.

Instead, for the first time since Tuesday's presentation, his shoulders came down from around his ears, and stayed down.

Chapter 2

The Light

It came to him on the bus, uninvited, somewhere between the last inland town and the sea. He had been imagining it for two days without admitting it - a tower, a lamp, a beam turning through fog like something in a film. The postcard version. The version that could be fixed, photographed, sold.

The air changed first. Salt - real this time, not the half-second ghost of the elevator. It came through the bus windows with the cold, and with it the sound he had forgotten he knew: gulls, close and quarrelsome. His jacket pocket held the letter's weight against his chest. Pat, pat. Read once. Still memorized. Still not understood.

Bellweather announced itself the way small towns do: a bent sign, a gas station, houses leaning toward the water as if listening. Lights were coming on in windows, one by one, though the afternoon was barely gone - people here prepared for evening early. A painted sign for a café swung in the wind. He glimpsed it all through glass and did not get off - the bus went on, past the town's edge, up the coast road. Nobody got on. The driver did not ask where he was headed. There was only one thing up that road.

He reached into his coat for the clicker and found the pocket empty. He checked the other pocket. The bag. Gone - left on the train's fold-down tray, two hundred miles south, probably already swept into some cleaner's bin. He waited for the irritation. It came, briefly, the way a wave comes: and then it went. He had owned it for years and used it for months. It had commanded rooms. Now it commanded a landfill. The arithmetic of that did not disturb him as much as it should have.

The bus rounded the headland, and there it was.

The lighthouse stood on black rock above a white-lined shore, gray stone going dark at the edges with the evening. Gulls wheeled around its crown. The lamp room was empty - no glass flash, no turning beam, nothing but a hollow where light used to live. It did not shine. It did not signal. It simply stood there, useless and exact, and it had been doing that for years.

The bus drove on. Elias turned in his seat and watched it recede, smaller and smaller, giving no light to anyone.

He got off at the next stop.

Chapter 3Bellweather
 

The Town

The stop was a bench, a shelter, and a schedule worn off. Elias found the town before he found the road to it.

Bellweather was smaller than the word. One street down to the water and one back. A cafe with a hand-painted sign. A hardware store with a window full of things that stopped being made. Laundry on a line, moving slightly, in no hurry. A boy on a bicycle going nowhere at a speed that suggested he had nowhere to be and had not decided yet.

Nobody looked at him. That was the first thing he noticed.

He had a coat that did not belong here and shoes that had never been on gravel, and the stillness of a man standing in a place with no use for him.

He got out his phone. There was no signal. He put it back.

Then he did the thing his hands had been trained to do for fourteen hours while the rest of him waited: he made a list.

Lighthouse. Access. Condition. Estimate.

Four lines. It took him ninety seconds, and it was a relief he would admit to no one. The town went back to being scenery. The boy on the bicycle stopped being a boy with a whole life and became a fact about the town's population density.

He finished the list. There was nothing else to add.

He had never in his life not known what the next line was.

He walked down the hill toward the water, because the lighthouse was that way, and because it was the only errand he had brought to this town.

Chapter 3

The Door

In daylight the lighthouse was not what the postcard had promised. The postcard had been his, or someone else's, printed with the tower straighter than this and the rock more dramatic. This one leaned very slightly, the way old things do. The white paint on the seaward side had gone the color of a bone.

It was smaller than it looked from a bus. From the road you expected something to look up to.

He walked around it first, the way you walk around a car you are thinking about buying. He put his hand flat on the stone. It was cold in a way that went deeper than the air. He checked the mortar by pressing the stones in sequence, the way he had learned to check buildings he was only ever going to write about - twelve years of advising on infrastructure, and he could not have built most of what he had inspected, but he knew how things failed, and it was the failing he was reading.

Forty years of weather, and nothing that could not be waited out.

The door was unlocked.

He tried it twice, so that he could say he had tried it twice. It swung inward a foot and stopped on stone, the way a door does when nobody has oiled the hinges in a long time.

He stood there with his hand on it.

Not hesitation. Nothing in his life had ever required hesitation. There was a door, a door was a problem, and problems were the only thing he had ever been reliably able to carry.

He should have waited for someone to come. He did not know who he was waiting for, and he was not a man who waited.

He went in.

Chapter 3

The Lighthouse

The inside smelled of cold stone and old iron and, faintly, of paper. His breath went up and found the dark ceiling before it came back down to him.

He turned on the phone's light, saw how stupid it looked in his hand, and put it away. The light through the slot windows was enough, once his eyes gave up arguing. That took longer than he expected, and he resented it.

The stairs went up. He counted them going, because counting was what he did instead of looking. Twenty-nine to the landing. He went up to the thirty-first and sat down, because thirty-one was where counting stopped feeling like counting, and he wanted to see whether it would start again on its own.

It did not.

Above him the stairs kept going. A lamp, a lens, and whatever it was that turned them, and however many pounds of glass it took to turn a beam that told the dark where the rocks were. He did not go and look. The job was not curiosity, and he was very good at jobs.

So he came back down instead.

And going down he passed the thing under the cloth at the foot of the stairs, and it was round, and it was not broken, and it was therefore not his problem. He had a career in faults. He could work with a fault.

By the time he reached the door he had already begun the second list.

Access. Water. Roof. Two weeks, maybe three.

He wrote it with his thumb in the air against the doorframe. The doorframe did not keep it. He made a real one on his phone, walking down to the street, where the signal was somehow worse.

Chapter 3

Mara

Six tables, four of them occupied. Behind the counter a woman who was not young and did not perform it: flour on one forearm, and a way of standing that said she had been there before the town woke up.

She did not smile at him. She looked at his shoes, then at his face, making whatever calculation people make when they decide how much of themselves to spend on a stranger. Then she picked up a cloth and wiped a counter that was already clean.

"Coffee," she said. "Two dollars. It's the only thing we have."

He put a five on the counter. She looked at it, then at him, and did not smile.

"Change comes back," she said, "or I come after you."

"It's fine," Elias said.

"It's not about fine," she said. "It's about counting."

He almost laughed. She saw that he almost had, and went back to the counter. That was the whole first conversation.

He asked her name on the way out, because he asked.

"Mara," she said, and kept wiping.

Forty, he guessed, and filed her under people who were pleasant.

He drank it standing up; sitting down would have made it an event. She refilled the cup without being asked, set it down, and did not fill the silence. After a moment she tipped her head toward the hill.

"You came up that road to look at that."

"It is," Elias said. "That's exactly what I came to ask."

She looked at him slightly too long, and then back at her counter.

"Then you're going to want to talk to people about it," she said, "and there's nobody here to talk to about it. That's not an accident."

Someone came in. She took his order without writing it down.

He walked out with the cup he had not asked for. Behind him the door shut. Down the hill the lighthouse stood doing nothing, and the door at the bottom of it was still open.

Chapter 3

The Clock

He went back up the hill.

He could not have said why. There was the coffee, there was the letter lying against his chest, and there was nothing to be done in any of it.

The door was still open. He had half-closed it behind him, or not - he could not remember - and there it was, a foot wide, patient, exactly as indifferent to whether he returned as when he left.

He did not count the stairs this time. He noticed that he did not count them, and then he noticed nothing else for a while.

The thing under the cloth at the foot of the stairs was a clock.

He turned the corner of the cloth back and saw the case, and the glass, and the hands where hands should be, and something about the way the hands had been left made him stop for slightly too long.

The face said nine-forty. The hands were not broken. They were resting at nine-forty the way two fingers rest on a table, and they had been resting there for years, and no one had ever come to move them.

He did not wind it. He did not test it. He stood in the cold holding a cloth nobody had given him permission to touch, waiting for himself to want to fix it. Nothing came.

Then he pulled the cloth back over it, the way you close a door in someone else's house, and went up.

Chapter 3

The Notebook

On the fourth step above the landing, where a person would sit to look at the water, there was a notebook.

It was not new. The cover had gone soft at the corners, and a rubber band around it had been stretched and had tried to return to shape and had only half managed.

The first page said, in a hand that started out careful and got less careful across one line and then started being careful again:

September 3rd. Bought lamp oil, 2 galls. The pump at the head of the road is slow - take the bucket.

He read it twice.

It was about lamp oil and a slow pump, and a man who wrote the date at the top because he was that kind of man.

He looked for the next one. There was not one on the page. There was one on the next page, and the one after that. Three hundred pages of a man's days, each of them one line, each beginning with the date, none of them written for him.

He sat down on the fourth step and read until the light went out of the slot window and the sea came up in the dark.

Somewhere in those three hundred pages, his uncle had written down the thing that explained all of it.

He had not found it yet. He was not even sure, sitting there with sand on his trousers, that it was in there at all.

He closed the notebook. He put it back on the step, exactly where it had been, in case that mattered to somebody. Then he took it with him.

Chapter 4The Notebook
 

The Pages

The tower had no chair and no lamp and no reason to be sat in after dark, and he sat in it anyway, with his back against the cold wall and the notebook open on his knees and the phone in his hand, its light making a small white room that moved when he moved.

He read the way he did everything.

He was through the first month before he understood that he had been reading.

September 8th. Council met. Nothing decided. Same men, same chairs.

September 19th. Replaced the gasket on the third door. It is the worst door I have ever worked on. It is also the only one I will not be able to close in a gale.

October 2nd. Twenty-eight degrees. The metal stair holds the cold the way a hand holds a stone.

October 6th. Wind over the top all afternoon. Spent the better part of an hour on the third door and got nowhere with it. A thing on the other side of a door is a thing you look at from the outside.

October 11th. Wrote four pages on the subject of light and could not get one of them to say anything. Burned them. The stove wanted them more.

He skimmed. That was the correct word and he would not have corrected it if someone had used a better one. Sixty pages went the way a memo went - supplies, weather, a complaint about the council, a gasket, a hinge, the price of lamp oil at three different shops and the theft of exactly none of it.

He was looking for the shape of the thing. Every notebook has one, and if you hold the pages back far enough and squint, it comes up out of the entries like a wire pulled out of a wall.

He did not find it in the first hundred pages. He expected to. He was also, he would have admitted, hoping to be disappointed, because being disappointed would mean the reading had cost him something and he could put a price on it.

The cold came up through the stone into his back. He shifted, and the notebook slid off his knees, and he caught it against his chest, and his hands were grateful for something to do.

Chapter 4

The Heron

He was a hundred and forty pages in before he slowed down, and he did not notice himself do it.

November 4th. Gulls on the roof again. Same one, missing the tip of the left wing.

January 12th. Eleven knots from the southwest. The whole building talking about it and not one word to me.

March 19th. The heron stood in the shallows for forty minutes. Did not move once. I timed it because I had nothing else to do.

He read it, and he read it again, and he was annoyed the second time, because the second time was supposed to be the one where the meaning arrived and it had not.

Nothing was in it. No instruction. No confession. A bird had stood in cold water for forty minutes and a man had counted, and the counting was admitted to be idle, and neither party had pretended otherwise.

He waited for a page.

He went looking for one, and he could not have told anyone the honest part: he was not reading his uncle's life, he was shopping in it. He had come in with a list of what he needed and he was going through the shelves.

He went back and read the heron a third time. The fourth time he only looked at it, at the small careful f of the date, and at the word nothing sitting in the middle of a sentence about a man with nothing to do.

Then he decided what it meant.

He decided his uncle had been bored. He decided forty minutes was a long time to stand in cold water, and that most men would have filled it, and that Thomas had not filled it.

He decided it in about four seconds, and he was satisfied, and the satisfaction was the sound a lock makes when it turns.

Then he read the next page, and the one after that, and the pages went by again at the speed he had come in at, and the heron went where the shape of the thing usually went: somewhere behind him, where he would find it later if he ever went looking.

Chapter 4

The Arithmetic

By three he had given up on the shape of the thing and started, instead, to do arithmetic.

Not on purpose. It happened the way his hands happened.

Supplies, then. He tallied them without meaning to, the way a man counts the planks on a dock. Lamp oil, forty gallons the first year and less after, because the second year he learned to burn less. Salt by the bag, always the same supplier. Gaskets, four. A hand drill, gone brittle, replaced. A box of rubber grommets he could not imagine the purpose of and refused to leave behind.

Repairs, in order, with the years running down the page like water.

And then, because the arithmetic wanted it, weather. Three hundred days of wind speed and direction, written down in a hand that did not vary, a man recording the sky the way an accountant records a ledger.

It was, Elias had to admit, an extraordinary amount of work.

It was also, he understood by four, not a life.

He tried to make it one. He went back three pages and read an entry about a storm in November where the words he and I both appeared, and it turned out to be the tide he, and he had read it as a man standing in weather with another man beside him.

There was no other man. There were three hundred pages and one hand.

He sat with the phone light in his lap and the notebook open on his knees and did the thing he had done at the office a hundred times. What did he have. What could he use. What was the next line.

What he had was a dead man's inventory, and there was not one item on the list that he could do anything with.

The cold had got into his hands. He shut the notebook on his thumb to keep the page and it closed itself somewhere in the second winter, and he lost his place, and he did not open it again for a while.

Chapter 4

The Gap

He opened it again at the wrong place and went looking for the entries he had already stopped reading.

He found it because he was a reader of gaps.

September 9th, first year. WOUND THE CLOCK. Secondhand store, forty miles. Man named Devlin, no teeth, kind about it.

October 30th. WOUND THE CLOCK. Seventeen days since I last wound it. I am not a patient man and this is not a patient clock.

April 2nd. WOUND THE CLOCK. Winding it twice now, which I gather is not the done thing.

October 14th. WOUND THE CLOCK. Twenty-six days this time. Something in the second movement. I will find it.

Then September of the next year, and nothing.

He went forward carefully, the way you go forward on ice you have not tested. No entry about the clock. He read a hundred pages looking for the word wound and the word clock and there was nothing, and the pages went on being what they had been: oil, salt, gulls, a council, a door that would not shut in a gale.

And Thomas went on writing.

That was the part he had to put the notebook down for.

He set it on the step, and got up, and his knees took a moment to decide about it, and the tide had come in while he was not looking and was going out again with a sound like something being agreed to.

He had stopped winding the clock and he had not stopped.

Twelve years of pages. He found the last one without hunting, the way you find a step you have taken a thousand times in the dark.

March 3rd. Nothing to report.

And then a page that was not dated at all, and the hand on it was the hand of a man who had stopped checking his work.

He got up to find out what time it was, and there was nothing in the room that told him.

Chapter 5The Clock
 

The Bench

A bench, first. A flat surface at the right height, with a lip, so the parts did not walk off it and get lost in a tower that already had a floor made of small stones.

He had not slept and was not going to. The morning came anyway, the way it does on that coast, out of a low grey nothing with no particular promise in it, and it found him still sitting on the fourth step with his back against the wall.

The cafe had a bench outside it that nobody used, because the wind came off the water and came straight through the slats. He moved three lengths of it into the lee of the seawall, wiped them down with his sleeve, and stood looking at the result as though he had built something.

He had done this kind of work before, in other buildings, for other people, on a Saturday that his firm would have billed. Light, and a clean surface, and the parts laid out in the order they came off, so that putting them back was only ever the reverse of taking them off. You wanted to be unhurried. You wanted to be the only person in the room.

He was not in a room. He was on a coast in the wind with a bag he had bought that morning for eleven dollars and a torch that had come with it, and the light was the flat grey kind that does not commit, and he worked anyway.

Mara came out with two coffees while he was on his knees measuring the plinth.

She did not ask what he was doing. She set one of them down, took the lid off the other, and drank from it herself, standing, the way people stand when they are going to be there a while without admitting it.

"The cafe shuts at four," she said. "That's the light. Not mine."

"I have a torch."

"You have four kinds of weather and no roof," she said, and went back inside, and left the coffee where it was, which he drank because it was there and because saying thank you would have meant stopping.

Chapter 5

The Taking Apart

The case came off in four quarter-turns, because four quarter-turns is how the case came off, and there was nothing clever about it and nothing else it was going to be.

Under the case: a glass clock. Not a mechanism with a face on the front of it, but the real arrangement, the one that is done to no purpose except that it lets you see the work, which meant someone had built this to be looked at. Elias had a phrase for that in his head and did not use it.

He laid the pieces out left to right on the bench.

The movement was smaller than a dinner plate and heavier than it had any right to be. Brass, dulled to the colour of a key. Two barrels, a going train, an escape wheel he could not have named correctly and did not need to name. The whole thing was finished to a standard he recognised professionally.

There was no dust in it.

That was the first thing that made him stop, and he stood there with the plate in his hands.

Eight years. The last entry in the notebook with wound in it was eight years old. In eight years in a salt tower on this coast, a movement like this one would have gone to dust the way the third door had gone to rust. It had not. Somebody had been in here. Somebody had opened this case and looked at it and put it back, and had done it recently, and had not wound it.

He put the plate down and picked up the notebook, which was in his bag, and found the page he wanted without looking for it, because he had read it enough times to know where things were.

October 14th. WOUND THE CLOCK. Twenty-six days this time. Something in the second movement. I will find it.

Twenty-six days, and then nothing, and then eight years of nothing, and a movement in his hands with no dust on it.

He put the notebook back in the bag and picked up the plate again.

Chapter 5

No Fault

He was thorough past the point of sense, and he knew it, and he did it anyway.

He cleaned every part. He laid out what he had found in rows and looked at the rows. He put the movement back together and took it apart again, faster, to see whether he had missed a step, and he had not, because there was no step to miss. He found the pivot he had suspected was bent and it was not bent. He found the thing he was certain was the source of the noise on the second beat and it was the source of the noise on the second beat, and it was a good noise, and it was not a fault either.

At two o'clock he went over the whole of it with the notebook's entries beside him, matching the complaints to the parts, in case Thomas had left a note about what was wrong with it.

Thomas had. Four years before it stopped, in the middle of a page about nothing, among a list of things that had gone loose:

Clock gains about a minute a day. I have decided to live with it.

Elias read it four times.

He sat on the wall with the movement in his hands and a man he had never met doing arithmetic in his head about a minute a day, over the whole life of the clock, spread so thin across the years that no afternoon had ever felt it.

He put the movement down and picked it up again, and then he was aware that he was holding the same part he had been holding when he started, and he put it down and did not pick it up.

Chapter 5

Nine-Forty

He put it back together. That took eleven minutes and he did it in the order he had learned it in, which was the reverse, and the case went on in four quarter-turns and the glass was clean and the hands were where the hands had been.

Nine-forty.

He got the winding key out of his bag, which is where it had been since the morning, because he had known all along that he was going to need it.

He turned it in the keyway and stopped.

Not because of anything in the movement. The movement was exactly as it had been, sound, complete, willing. He stopped because starting it would have been a decision, and he had not come here to make one, and he could not have said who the decision belonged to.

It was not his. It took him a while to get to the bottom of that, because for nine years he had thought everything within reach was his.

He put the key in his pocket. He pulled the cloth back over it, and squared the cloth on the case the way it had been, and left it the way he had found it except for the four quarter-turns, which nobody would ever know about.

His hands smelled of oil. He wiped them on his jeans and looked at the bench, at the parts laid out in rows in the order they came off, and picked them up one at a time and put the case over them and carried the whole thing back to the foot of the stairs in two trips, because he would not trust himself to the stairs with both hands full.

Down at the rail halt there was a boy.

He was about seventeen and he had a bicycle leaned against the fence, and he was holding a length of rail in one hand the way somebody holds a stick they have just finished arguing with, and he said, without any preamble at all:

"You've had that open."

Chapter 6The Boy Who Fixed Things Nobody Used
 

The Boy

"I have," Elias said.

The boy did not introduce himself. He was perhaps seventeen, with the shoulders of somebody who had been doing something with his hands since he was eleven, and a way of standing on a rail line that suggested the line was his and Elias was on it.

Elias put his hand out.

"Jonah," the boy said, and did not take it, and after a moment the hand came back down and went into his pocket, and there was nothing wrong with the gesture and nothing Elias could do about it.

"You're the one with the suit on a Tuesday," Jonah said. "You were in the tower last night. I saw a light go up in the tower about eleven and go off about two."

Elias had a great deal of practice at being the most capable person in a conversation, and the first four seconds in this one were the longest of his adult life.

"Have you been up there," he said.

"Once."

"Recently?"

"First week of summer," Jonah said. "The door's been like that since I was about twelve." He looked at Elias with no expression in particular. "It's not broken, your clock."

"I know it's not broken. I had it open all day yesterday. I took the movement out and there isn't anything wrong with it anywhere."

"Then what are you doing up there."

It was not rudeness. The boy was asking a question, because he wanted the answer.

"I'm restoring it," Elias said. "It was my uncle's. There's a condition attached to it."

"What condition."

"That I put it right before it's sold."

Jonah absorbed this and moved on without any comment, and then he nodded up the line toward the headland, at nothing at all, and started walking, and Elias discovered that the conversation had been reassigned.

Chapter 6

The Line

The line came in off the coast road and stopped. Not degraded - stopped. The rails went past the platform a hundred yards and then stopped too, two bright scars in the ballast where the ends had been cut, and a gate in a fence that had been open since before Jonah was born.

Four hundred yards of it was his. His grandfather had left it to him in a will that had gone through a solicitor in town and had cost somebody eleven pounds, and it had stopped at four hundred yards for a reason the solicitor's letter had explained and Jonah could not now remember. He said so the way you say the time.

"There's another mile and a bit north," he said. "That's not mine. That belongs to nobody, which is a different thing, and I haven't got to it."

"Why are you doing this?"

Jonah levered the bar under a sleeper and did not answer for long enough that Elias thought he had decided not to. Then:

"It was broke."

"Things are broken."

Elias had nothing to say to that. The silence went on about two seconds longer than he had planned for, and the boy's hands kept moving the whole time he was not talking.

Elias made the mistake at about the fourth minute, the way he made all of them - smoothly, and in front of people.

"How much rail have you got through?"

"Forty foot of proper and about ninety of the light stuff. Rail is the expensive bit. Sleepers are cheap if you're patient, because there's a yard down the road where they burn a lot of nothing, and a man there lets me take them if I stack them."

"That's not a bad arrangement."

"It's what there is."

"There are better arrangements. There is a supply chain for this - you could buy new sleepers by the pallet, and there's no reason to be taking them off a bonfire. If somebody put a schedule to this, a real one, you'd have this section done by the end of next summer instead of-"

"End of what," Jonah said, and looked up.

Elias stopped, because he had walked into a door in a town where he did not know where all the doors were.

"Out of business?" Jonah said. "That's what you were going to say. End of next summer, out of business, and you're the guy who builds schedules." He went back to the sleeper. "I don't do next summer. I do the bit in front of me."

Chapter 6

The Hands

The end of the twenty yards had eight sleepers out and eleven to go, and Jonah worked from the far end back toward the tower, and Elias knew it was wrong inside about a minute and a half of watching.

"You want the closed end first," he said. "You'll end up with a crown that'll move."

"It's how the last one was done."

"Yeah." Jonah pulled. "Look at the last one."

It took Elias until the third row to be honest with himself about what was happening, and by then it had been going on for a while.

Jonah was good. He was better than good. He did not waste a movement and he did not speak while he worked, and twice he had to reposition Elias - not with words, by taking the bar out of his hands and putting it back down somewhere else - and the second time he said "there" and Elias said "I knew that" and Jonah went on.

They got the eight rows down in about an hour. Elias was, without any question, the fitter man. He had spent twelve years keeping his hands in shape for a job that mostly required neither, and he had never once had a use for them, and now he did, and he found the discovery almost physically hard to sit in.

He was also, for about forty minutes, happy. Not moved. Not grateful. He was a man with a bar and a competent boy and a thing in front of him, on a Thursday, and his hands were full.

Jonah put the tools in a bag and shouldered the rail and started walking.

"I'm going up the road to the yard," he said. "If you want the last eleven, come at seven in the morning. Not before. I've got a school thing."

And then he stopped, and did not turn round, and said:

"You've oil in your hands still. From yesterday. You going to do something with that clock, or not?"

Chapter 6

What It Is Worth

The number came up on the third morning, because Jonah asked, and it came up the way numbers did with Elias, instantly and without ceremony.

"There's a buyer for the tower," Elias said. "The harbour trust won't pay much, it's a wreck, but the frontage is worth having. Somewhere north of two hundred and fifty, and they've told the lawyer twice, which means nearer three. They've asked twice, through the lawyer, and I have not said no to either of them."

Jonah was sitting on the end of a rail with his gloves off.

"Right," he said.

"You could finish your line for that."

"That's not why I told you."

"I know. I'm telling you it's why a man in your position would have told you. It's a fact about the value of the thing I've come here to destroy." Elias heard himself, and stopped, and they both let it stand there. "Sorry. That came out wrong."

"No," Jonah said. "It came out the way it came out. I've been out here since May. I know what a number is." He put his gloves back on.

Elias understood, late and all at once, that he had described himself to a seventeen-year-old, and the boy had put the description down and gone back to work.

"Forty foot of proper rail," Jonah said, to the sleeper, "is about nine hundred. Depending. There's a man at the yard sells it by the length and he's got four hundred foot of the light stuff in the back and he'll take six a metre for it if you ask him nice, and I haven't asked him nice yet, because I haven't got six."

"You could have six."

"I could have six." Jonah levered the bar. "Then I've got no line, because six is a metre of track and I'd spend it in a week and be back where I am. I want the whole mile. That's the whole point. If I take six I can stop and say I started."

Elias had no objection to make, and he had none to make, and a boy who was about to be handed three hundred thousand pounds had just said, without any self-pity in it, that he would rather stay hungry than let the hunger stop.

"Which man's going to be asking you nice," Elias said.

Jonah looked up the coast road towards the harbour end of town, and the look lasted about a second longer than a look at a road usually does.

"Pell," he said. "He'll be at the tower by now. He's up there most mornings. He talks a lot." The boy went back to the rail, and then, without any change of tone, and evidently deciding that Elias was owed it: "He says the light's going to be back on before Christmas. Every year."

Chapter 7The Work Begins
 

Pell

He was at the top of the coast road at seven, which was earlier than Elias had expected and later than he had hoped.

An old man with a boat's worth of rope in the bottom of a canvas bag and a folding spanner he did not appear to use for anything. He had a key, which was the first thing he mentioned.

"You'll be the one that's had my clock open," Pell said.

"You've had a key to it for how long?"

"Long enough." He said it the way you say a price. Then he looked at the tower for a while, the way a man looks at something that is his problem, and said: "That mortar's failed."

"While you've had the key," Elias said. "Have you had it open?"

The old man took the bag off his shoulder and set it down on the wall.

"Last winter. November, and it was a bad one." He said it without any weight at all, the way a man reports a thing he has never once thought about. "There's no dust in it. I noticed that then as well. Looked in, couldn't see the point of it, shut it again. Whatever you're looking for in there, it isn't a thing that's gone off."

"It's been going for eight years."

"Then whoever was last at it was more careful than me." Pell picked the bag up again. "I'll not have you thinking I'd be the one that stopped a thing."

He looked at the tower for a while longer, and then: "That mortar's failed."

Elias did not answer the second time either. The silence went on a beat too long, and then the old man said, "Well," and went through the door, which was apparently a fact about the world rather than a condition of it.

Inside, on the off-shore wall, Elias put his thumb into a joint where the pointing had gone soft and crumbly, and it crumbled, and it had been crumbling for years, and it would go on crumbling for years more unless somebody spent a week on it with a bucket and a trowel and got paid nothing for the week.

He had told himself, on the train, on the strength of one afternoon with his hands flat on the stone, that there was very little wrong with this building. He had been right. Forty years of weather and nothing that could not be waited out.

The old man was wrong about the mortar.

Elias did not say so, and then he heard himself almost say so, and then he did not say so at all, because the old man had a key and the tower was full of damp and there was going to be a great deal of work in the next fortnight, and it was not worth the trouble to be right about a wall with a man who was on his side.

He was on his side. Nobody ever told you that the person who was wrong would also be the one who handed you a key.

Chapter 7

The Drain

The old man was right about the drain.

That was the second morning, and Elias had gone up alone, and he had spent an hour and a half on the gallery with a wire brush and a bucket, clearing the channel where the water ran off the outside, and the water had not stopped, and he had begun to suspect the pipe rather than the channel, and the old man had come up behind him and said, without any pleasure in it at all:

"Pipe's fine. Look up."

The pipe was fine. Two floors up, on the seaward side, the gallery gutter had packed solid with eight years of gull and salt and the small green things that grow in a wet gutter, and it had been overflowing since before Elias arrived, quietly, every time it rained, into the top of the lamp room, where the ceiling had a bloom on it the shape of a country neither of them could name.

Elias stood in a tower and looked at a ceiling going soft.

"Right," he said.

"Yes," said Pell. "That's the year of it."

The old man could have said anything he liked about the Saturday morning a stranger had spent six days in his tower. He did not. He got a ladder, and he went up the ladder, and he was slower than Elias on it and steadier, and for the rest of the morning the two of them worked the gutter out with a bucket between them, passing it hand to hand up the ladder, and neither of them said anything at all for about an hour.

When it was done Elias took out his wallet.

The old man looked at it, and then at him, and then at the ladder, as though the ladder had said something funny.

"No," he said.

"It's a day's work."

"It's a day's work I wanted to do." He shouldered the bucket. "You'll want to pay me for the second storey, mind. When you do it, and you'll not be able to do it the same way, because a man can't get his whole arm round the outside of that bend."

He went down the ladder, and stopped, and said: "You'll get it wrong, then. Everybody does. You'll get it wrong and you'll have to go out on a rope, and I know a man for the rope, and he's dearer than me."

Chapter 7

The Third Door

The third door.

Thomas had written about it four times, which was twice as often as anything else in three hundred pages, and the last word on it, from a night in his fourth winter, was this:

Third door. It is the worst door I have ever worked on. It is also the only one I will not be able to close in a gale.

Elias read the line on the fourth morning, standing up, with the notebook in one hand, and then he went down and got the tools and took the door off its hinges.

That was the whole of it. Two new hinges, because the old ones had been welded into the wood by somebody in a temper a long time ago, and they came off with a grinder and a bit of swearing, and the frame had to be chiselled back to bare wood and treated and left overnight, and the door went back up on the second afternoon, and he hung it on the new hinges, and it closed.

It took a pull to open and a push to shut and it shut properly, all the way, on the first try, and the noise it made was small and dry and completely unremarkable.

He stood in the corridor of his dead uncle's lighthouse with his hand flat on a door he had closed, and waited.

Nothing happened. He was waiting for something and could not have said what, and it had not been about the door, and for about four seconds he was not sure whether you have always been good at solving things had been an instruction or an apology.

He went back up the stairs and wrote it on his phone: Third door. Done.

He looked at the two words and then at the sentence above them, which said two weeks, maybe three, and he thought about how many days it had actually been, and about the list behind the list, and about the fact that he had come here to assess a thing and was now on his fourth morning with a grinder and a box of hinges and a door that shut.

He went to look at the gutter.

Chapter 7

The Estimate

He did the estimate properly. He sat on the wall where the bench had been, in the evening, and he went through it line by line the way he would have gone through a budget, and he made it worse.

Lamp room, gallery, lantern, third stair: the sea air has had sixty years. Call it two weeks with two men and a hoist I don't have.

Roof and lantern lead: not a fortnight. A month, and I don't know whose trade that is.

Gutter and downpipe, all elevations: a week, and the third elevation needs a rope.

Interior, plaster, floors, the small door: two months and I will be guessing by the second.

He read it back. It was not two weeks, maybe three. It was closer to five months of work, and then the sale after that, which was in every version of the number and on none of the lines, and every item on it was a trade he did not have, and the hoist and the rope man and the third elevation were three separate strangers he would have to go and find, and he had not found one of them yet, and there was no machinery on this coast except Jonah's bicycle and a bus that ran twice a day.

He did it again the next evening, and got four months, which was a different number and not a better one.

He sat with the two estimates. Two weeks. Four months. The building had not changed. The sea air had not changed. The trades he did not have were the same trades. The only thing that had changed was the number, and he could not decide what the difference meant.

He had done this for a living. He had a name for the way of thinking and the name was worse than the thinking.

He put the phone away. He went up and looked at the third door from the inside, where it looked like any other door, and it was shut, and he had made it shut, and it had taken two days.

The leaves were not going to turn for a long time yet.

Chapter 8The Things People Stay For
 

The Bill

He had been eating at the cafe every day for nine days, and on the tenth she put the tray down and put her hand flat on it.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked."

"You've been doing the face at the door all week." She took the tray back. "There's four of you lot now. The rail boy's been in three times and he can't afford eggs, don't tell him I said that. You can have yours on the slate."

"Mara-"

"You can have yours on the slate," she said, "and at the end of it you can do the work, which you would have done anyway, and then you can tell yourself you settled up."

He looked at the slate, and at her, and had the sense to be quiet.

He ate. He was fed on a slate that was not a bill and was not charity, and twice a day she fed four men who were putting a lighthouse back together, and none of it was discussed, and once a week she wiped the total off the board without asking him for anything, and he never once worked out what she was actually doing with the arithmetic.

Pell came at seven. Jonah came when he came. Dougie did the roof lead in four days and charged by the day and was the cheapest competent person Elias had ever met in his life.

The schedule sat in his bag, ticks stopping at day eleven. He did not cross them out. He just stopped opening it.

Chapter 8

The List

"You don't get to do that," she said.

"Do what."

"Ask me that like it's a question with a bottom to it." She put a spoon in the sink. "You've got a face on you like a man doing a survey. Ask me something you actually want to know."

Elias had no technique for being told off by a woman with her back to him.

"Why do you stay," he said.

She ran the tap for a while.

"The bread," she said. "There's a bakery up past the post office, it goes stale by four, and if I'm here I get it hot. If I'm not, whoever gets it is standing in that queue at half three like everybody else. That's one."

"That isn't why."

"No. Next."

"Your dog."

"Boat." She said it without turning round. "There's nothing wrong with the dog. I love the dog. The dog would be fine anywhere. Don't put the dog in it."

Elias put his hands flat on the table, the posture he had used on the fourteenth floor for two decades, and asked the next question, and then the next.

"The tide comes in at half past four and out at nine. There's a stretch of wall you can only get to on the low, and a man comes for crabs who'll only come at low water. I know he's there. That's a reason, that."

"The bus goes at ten past seven and it's the last one, and I've missed it four times in as long as I've been counting, and every single time I was on that road after dark with nothing to think about."

"How long have you been here?"

"Forty-one years, next March."

She turned the tap off, dried her hands, and sat down opposite him for the first time in ten days.

"There's the light," she said. "I do the lamp. Nobody's allowed to show it, which is a different thing, and I haven't stopped. I do it because Pell can't be trusted with a ladder and because your uncle didn't want it done by nobody, and because if the light's out then the harbour trust can say it's derelict and sell it to anybody with a wire, and there's a man in this town I would rather not think about saying that." She put her cup down. "They've written to you twice, by the way. It's in the flat above the shop, I saw the envelope when I was up there for the bins." She did not look up from the cup. "So that's four. Is that enough for you?"

"Yes."

"It isn't a reason," she said. "It's a Tuesday."

Chapter 8

The Arithmetic

"I'd like to do it properly," Elias said. "If you'll let me."

Mara put her head on one side and regarded him the way you regard a dog that has brought you something.

"Properly how."

"You've thought about leaving. Forty-one years is not a decision, it's a drift. If you sat down and did it honestly - the money, the trade, what you'd actually want - you'd find out whether you have an answer or whether you've just never been asked the question."

"And if I did it honestly?"

"Then you'd know."

"Brilliant," she said. "You've invented a survey. Of me."

He had opened his bag. There was a notebook in it that was not the notebook, and she looked at it, and he put his hand flat on it, and did not open it.

"I do it twice a year," she said.

The tower made a sound above them, the way it did in a westerly, a long settling in the stone. She waited for it.

"Twice a year I do the sums. Train's a hundred and forty if you book it. There's a place in my sister's town that would take me on, she's asked me twice and she means it. The sums say go. The sums have said go for eleven years."

"And?"

"And then it's March, and the light's coming back on, and there's four of you lot on that hill and nobody doing the roof properly." She turned her cup round on the table. "So I don't go, and I don't say out loud that I'm not going, because then it's a decision, and then I've made it and I'd have to keep making it."

Elias sat with that, and felt the ground of the conversation shift under him.

"That's not drift," he said. "That's a decision you keep not making."

"Yes," she said. "Thank you. That's exactly what I said, and I've been saying it to myself for eleven years, and you did it in about four minutes and now what."

Chapter 8

A Small Life

He thought about it all afternoon on the roof, on his own, with Dougie's lead open and the wind coming round the lantern, and at about four o'clock he took the notebook out and wrote her down.

Name. Age, guessed. Time here - forty-one. Bakery, tide, bus, dog, sister, a man for the crabs on the low water. The lamp, which nobody is allowed to show. Reason given: a Tuesday.

Nine days at a table and it had taken him ninety seconds. He read it back and it was a good entry and it was complete, and it was the only thing he had written in the notebook in ten days, and it was not a note about the tower.

There was a great deal of daylight left. From where he sat the front of the cafe and about nine feet of the ground behind it were visible, and he watched her for the rest of it without meaning to, the way he had watched the town on his first afternoon.

At twenty to five she came out from behind the building with a galvanised bucket and went along the wall to where the tide was out, and crouched down at the third step, and stayed down there about a quarter of an hour. From the top of a lighthouse you cannot see what a person is doing when they are crouching. He watched her do it anyway. Chapter 9

You Look Different

Chapter 10The Notebook's Last Pages
 

Less

February 14th. Nothing doing. Gasket on the third door again. It is the third door.

February 27th. Nothing doing.

Chapter 10

The Door

Chapter 10

Not Understanding

He wanted, very badly, for the notebook to be about something.

That was the whole of it, and he could see it happening. There wasn't one.

Twice, in three hundred pages, he had written down something about himself, and both times it was the body or the day and not the thought:

Knees bad this winter.

Lonely on a Tuesday. Bed early.

Elias put the notebook down on the step, and put his head back against the wall, and said, out loud, to nobody:

"I don't know him."

Chapter 10

The Colour It Goes

He read the undated page once more in the morning, on the step, with the coffee going cold on the wall beside him, and it said the same thing it had said the night before, and he put the notebook in his bag rather than back on the step, which he noticed himself doing.

He stayed up there longer than the job took, and he could not have said why, and the light was going strange in the way it does when a front is coming up from the south-west, and he found that he was looking for a long time at a stretch of water he had not looked at properly in a month.

He came down the ladder with the trowel in his hand and stood in the yard, and the wind came round the corner of the building and made that noise in the stone, and it was a much bigger noise than it made in an ordinary westerly, and it had a direction to it.

Chapter 11The Storm
 

Coming

Pell came at seven with his bag of rope and did not get out of the car. It was a Wednesday, and the morning was the same as the morning before it, and the morning before that.

"This'll be here by two," he said. "I'd go down to the harbour if I were you. Everybody'll go down to the harbour."

"Two hours is four hours."

"It's four hours if you leave at two."

Elias had spent six weeks on this coast and had learned three things about weather and none of them were about severity. He had learned that it announces itself, that it is not punctual, and that the light goes strange in the south-west before anything else happens.

Pell sat in his car with the engine running.

"You'll not get the gallery door shut behind you in that," he said. "That latch has been a liar for twenty years."

The wind came round the corner of the building at about eleven and did not stop. It did not arrive, it did not rise and fall, it simply came, and it came from the south-west with the whole weight of the water behind it, and by noon the rain was going sideways off the sea and hitting the tower in a noise like gravel being thrown at a wall.

Chapter 11

The Noise It Makes

The stone talked.

Not a crack, not a report, not anything that could be located - the whole tower working, the way a big old thing works when there is a sea driving into the base of it, and Elias found that the noise made four separate sounds depending on which side of the building he was standing on.

He had been in the lantern for an hour when he noticed what he was actually doing, which was talking to himself. Not out loud. He was giving the wind a running total.

He stopped, and the wind took the silence and made it bigger, and he stood in a lantern room in the dark with his hands full of cold lead, and he had taken about a third of it, and the other two thirds had been under a cloth at the foot of the stairs.

No bars, and not the small empty circle that means searching, but the plain dead nothing of a coast in a westerly. Chapter 11

The Light

Chapter 11

The Boy

The Door

What It Cost

Chapter 13

The Flat

The Guitar

He got the plastic off on the fourth evening.

The Running One

They came down through the lamp room and Jonah stopped on the landing the way he always did, and the clock was on the wall with its glass off, and it was going.
